LabTrace

The labs you already have, finally legible.

An AI-powered personal lab record: upload any lab report from any provider, and a Claude-powered pipeline extracts every value, normalizes it to healthcare standards, and trends it over time against optimal ranges you define — then hands your doctor a one-page summary they can read in under a minute.

Status: In active development — shipping to TestFlight. Built solo with Claude Code. This page documents the working system and the engineering decisions behind it.

THE PROBLEM

I'm user zero. My lab history lives in six places: Quest, Labcorp, a consumer testing service, a retail clinic, a university health system, and a hospital dermatology department — plus continuous biometrics from an Oura ring. No portal talks to any other. Seeing my ferritin trend across two years means opening six tabs and a spreadsheet I maintain by hand. Every new provider visit starts with me verbally reconstructing my own medical history.

This isn't a niche problem. Patients re-test what they've already tested. Clinicians decide on partial pictures. And the people hit hardest are the ones who already struggle to be taken seriously in an exam room — showing up with clean, objective, longitudinal data changes that conversation. The integration burden shouldn't fall on the patient. Today it does, so most people give up.

The blocker was never ambition — it was data entry. Lab reports come in thousands of formats. Rule-based extraction never generalized. That just changed.

THE SYSTEM

Pipeline: document → structured record → trend → provider one-sheet

  1. 1. Ingest. Upload a PDF or photograph of any lab report. Any provider, any format, any angle.
  2. 2. Extract (Claude). The document goes to Claude under a strict structured-output schema: analyte, value, unit, printed reference range, collection date, source — extraction is verbatim-only, with a confidence rating per field. Output is validated against the schema before anything proceeds.
  3. 3. Normalize. Analytes map to LOINC codes (the universal lab-test vocabulary); units normalize via UCUM. A "ferritin" from Quest and a "FERRITIN, SERUM" from Labcorp become the same observation — which is what makes cross-source trending possible at all.
  4. 4. Human-in-the-loop review. Nothing saves silently. High-confidence fields pre-accept for one-tap confirmation; low-confidence fields are flagged for explicit review against the source image. In health data, wrong is worse than missing — the review step is a product requirement, not a UX nicety.
  5. 5. Trend. Every biomarker charts across all sources over time, color-coded against optimal ranges the user sets — because the targets that matter are the ones your own clinician gave you, not the population reference range printed on the report.
  6. 6. Share. A one-page provider summary — selected biomarkers, selected date range — designed to be scanned by a clinician in under sixty seconds. The export ends in a doctor's hand, not on your phone.

THE PART MOST AI APPS SKIP: EVALS

An AI product without measurement is a demo. LabTrace's launch is gated on a hand-labeled evaluation set of real lab documents, held out from prompt iteration, measuring field-level accuracy for values, units, dates, and analyte identification.

The gate isn't just headline accuracy — it's calibration. The entire human-review mechanism depends on one assumption: when the model says it's unsure, it should actually be more likely to be wrong. So calibration is itself a launch blocker. If user corrections don't concentrate on the fields the model flagged as low-confidence, the routing is unsound and the product doesn't ship — regardless of how good the accuracy number looks.

In production, the same instrumentation continues: correction rates, confidence-distribution drift, per-format accuracy. Lab formats change; model behavior gets monitored, not assumed.

WHAT THE PRODUCT DELIBERATELY DOESN'T DO

LabTrace does not diagnose. It does not recommend supplements or interventions. It does not suggest target ranges based on conditions. Users set their own ranges — typically the ones their own clinicians gave them.

These aren't missing features; they're gates. Condition-based recommendations move from "displaying your data" toward FDA clinical-decision-support territory, on a scientific evidence base that is genuinely contested. Those features stay on the roadmap behind explicit evidence and regulatory review — because in health software, the features you refuse to ship without proof are as much a part of the product as the ones you do.

THE ENTERPRISE TRACK

The same pipeline solves an enterprise problem: health systems receive patient-generated and outside lab data as unstructured documents that die as scanned images in the EHR. I've designed (and am building against Epic's public FHIR sandbox) a deployment architecture for exactly this — Claude extraction → LOINC/UCUM normalization → clinician verification queue → FHIR R4 Observations written to the EHR with full provenance, plus an AI-generated longitudinal summary at the point of care, with extraction-accuracy and verification-time metrics instrumented from day one.

Modeled on a healthcare enterprise client I supported during my consulting days, it's a working demonstration of the pattern I find most interesting in applied AI: putting a frontier model at the messy, unstructured boundary of an entrenched system — and meeting that system on its own standards rather than around them.
